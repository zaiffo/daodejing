# 第005-2章：不仁与刍狗

## 原文

### 第1页

“天地不仁，以万物为刍狗”很容易被读成一句冷酷的话：既然天地不偏爱谁，统治者是否也可以不顾具体人的痛苦？

法家对《老子》的吸收，使这个问题变得实际。相同的“无为”“不仁”，放在老子那里限制统治者的私意，放进君主权术以后，却可以用来要求臣民服从，并让君主更难被揣测。

### 第2页

差别可以先从“无为”看。老子要求主政者少以个人欲望扰动天下；韩非讨论君道时，则强调君主不要轻易显露好恶，让臣下无法预先迎合，再以名责实、按结果考核。

一个是在限制权力任性，一个是在提高权力的控制能力。用字相近，实际约束的对象已经不同。

## 释义

### 第3页

老子说“天地不仁”，不是说自然怀有恶意。雨落下来，不会先查谁贤谁不贤；寒暑到来，也不会因为某个人受人敬爱便为他停下。这里说的是不按私人感情改变运行，并不是命令人对苦难无动于衷。

一旦把两者混为一谈，“不偏私”便会被改写成“不必理会人的感受”。

### 第4页

“圣人不仁”同样不是要求统治者冷漠。亲近的人犯法，不能因为受宠便免罚；疏远的人有理，也不能因为没有门路便得不到处理。治理若随个人喜怒改变，今日的恩惠很快会成为明日的不安。

所以，不仁在这里首先要求去除偏爱。它仍需面对人的处境，也仍要说明规则为何如此、伤害如何补救。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第5页

“刍狗”是古代祭礼使用的草扎狗。仪式举行时，人们将它陈设好；仪式结束后，便不再供奉。庄子后来的相关叙述更清楚地写出它在仪式前后的不同待遇。这个形象提醒人不要把一时的尊奉误认成永久占有。

“橐籥”指古代风箱，中间空，拉动时才能送出空气。老子用它说明空处也能发生作用。这个比喻谈的是天地如何生发，并不直接给出一套政治制度。

## 正文

### 第6页

法家对“无为”的改写，首先落在君主如何面对臣下。君主不公开喜好，不亲自承担每项事务，而是让臣下陈述职责，再按结果核验。臣下难以用讨好换取信任，君主则保留最后的奖惩权。

这与老子的重点不同。老子要减少主政者的私意干扰；韩非更关心怎样让臣下无法欺骗主君。前者限制统治冲动，后者使统治更有效。

### 第7页

法家谈赏罚时，更关心命令能否执行。临时宽免可能被视为削弱法令，私人同情也可能被视为破坏威慑。商鞅的政治思路尤其强调让人口和资源服从耕战，使民众难以形成不受国家控制的力量。

这不是天地“不偏私”的自然秩序，而是国家有明确目的的组织与驱使。

### 第8页

“刍狗”也不能直接等同于百姓。老子借祭器说明天地无私，并没有要求统治者在使用之后抛弃人民。到了耕战国家的制度中，百姓却被登记、征发，并按能够提供的粮食与兵役计算。

韩非说“民之性，恶劳而乐佚”，由此主张以赏罚把人驱向农战。此时受到重视的，是国家从一个人身上能取得多少产出，而不是他能否按自己的方式生活。

### 第9页

《解老》《喻老》说明韩非并非偶然借用几个词。他系统地把老子的若干概念放进君臣关系：君主隐藏意图，占据权位，以规则和考核观察臣下。

读者因此要看清概念落在谁身上。若“无为”要求君主克制自己，它减少权力的扰动；若“无为”只要求君主不露声色，它也可能让权力更难受到监督。

### 第10页

秦朝展示了高度动员的两面。它迅速灭六国、统一度量衡，也征发大量人口承担军役与工程。传统叙事中，陈胜、吴广遇雨误期，担忧依秦法受罚，于是决定起兵。

秦亡不能归结为这一件事，也不能只归结为法家。但这个故事说明，法令若只规定期限和惩罚，却不给灾害造成的误期留下申诉办法，一场雨就可能把普通人的选择压缩到服刑、逃亡或反抗。

## 回扣

### 第11页

历史上确实有把人只按赋税、粮食和兵役计算的治理。那不是老子所谓“不仁”的直接结论。“不仁”要求公正地对待亲疏，“无为”要求主政者少以私欲扰民；二者都不能为任意处罚和隐秘操控提供理由。

### 第12页

区分老子与法家，不必停在“谁更温柔”。可以看三件具体的事：权力是否需要说明理由，受罚者是否能够申诉，规则是否也约束制定规则的人。

相同的古字进入不同制度，会产生不同生活。辨认这种差别，比把“不仁”简单解释成仁慈或冷酷更重要。

# Chapter 005-2: Non-Benevolence and Straw Dogs

## Original Text

### Page 1

“Heaven and earth are not benevolent; they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs” can easily sound cruel. If Heaven favors no one, may a ruler also ignore the suffering of particular people?

Legalist uses of Laozi make the question practical. The same words—non-action and non-benevolence—can restrain a ruler’s private will in one setting, yet in the language of statecraft help subjects obey and make the ruler harder to read.

### Page 2

The difference appears first in non-action. Laozi asks those who govern to disturb the world less with private desire. Han Feizi, writing about rulership, advises the sovereign not to reveal preferences too readily, lest ministers learn to flatter them, and to compare assigned duties with actual results.

One account restrains arbitrary power; the other increases power’s capacity for control. Similar language now governs a different person.

## Interpretation

### Page 3

When Laozi says that Heaven and earth are not benevolent, he does not mean that nature bears malice. Rain does not first ask who is virtuous, and the seasons do not stop for someone because that person is beloved. The line describes an order that does not change according to private affection; it does not instruct human beings to become indifferent to suffering.

Once those claims are confused, impartiality can be rewritten as permission not to care.

### Page 4

The sage’s non-benevolence is not an instruction to become cold. A favorite who breaks the law should not be excused because he is close to the ruler. A stranger with a valid grievance should not be ignored because she lacks access. When administration follows personal affection, today’s favor becomes tomorrow’s insecurity.

Non-benevolence first removes favoritism. It must still attend to human circumstances, explain rules, and provide remedies for harm.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 5

Straw dogs were ritual objects. Before the rite they were carefully arranged; afterward they were no longer revered. A related passage in the *Zhuangzi* makes this change of treatment especially clear. The image warns against mistaking temporary honor for permanent possession.

A bellows is empty in the middle and moves air when worked. Laozi uses it to show that an empty space can still have an effect. The image concerns the generative activity of Heaven and Earth; it does not by itself supply a political system.

## Main Text

### Page 6

The Legalist revision of non-action appears first in the ruler’s conduct toward ministers. The ruler withholds personal preferences, delegates tasks, and then tests claims against results. Ministers cannot rely as easily on pleasing him, while final reward and punishment remain in his hands.

Laozi’s emphasis is different. He seeks to reduce interference caused by the ruler’s private will. Han Feizi is more concerned with preventing ministers from deceiving the sovereign. The former restrains the urge to rule; the latter makes rule more effective.

### Page 7

Legalist discussions of reward and punishment focus on whether commands can be enforced. An exceptional pardon may weaken a statute; private sympathy may weaken deterrence. Shang Yang’s political program places particular weight on directing population and resources toward agriculture and war, while limiting independent sources of social power.

This is not the impartiality of the seasons. It is organization by a state with explicit purposes.

### Page 8

Nor should the straw dog simply be identified with the people. Laozi uses a ritual object to speak of Heaven’s lack of favoritism; he does not tell rulers to discard their subjects after use. In states organized for agriculture and war, however, people were registered, levied, and assessed by the grain and military service they could provide.

Han Feizi writes that people dislike labor and enjoy ease, and therefore argues for rewards and punishments that direct them toward farming and war. What matters here is how much the state can obtain from a person, not whether that person can shape a life of his own.

### Page 9

Han Feizi’s *Explaining Laozi* and *Illustrating Laozi* show that he did more than borrow a few words. He places several Daoist concepts within relations between ruler and minister: conceal intention, occupy the position of authority, assign duties, and examine performance.

We therefore need to ask whom a concept restrains. When non-action restrains the ruler, it reduces disturbance by power. When it means only that the ruler remains unreadable, it can also make power harder to supervise.

### Page 10

The Qin dynasty shows two sides of concentrated mobilization. It conquered the six states and standardized weights and measures, while conscripting large populations for military service and construction. In the traditional account, Chen Sheng and Wu Guang were delayed by rain, feared punishment under Qin law, and chose rebellion.

Qin’s fall cannot be reduced to this episode or to Legalism alone. The story does show what happens when law specifies deadlines and punishments but leaves no credible remedy for delay caused by disaster: one storm can narrow ordinary people’s options to punishment, flight, or revolt.

## Return to the Original

### Page 11

History does contain systems that counted people chiefly by tax, grain, and military service. That is not a direct conclusion from Laozi’s non-benevolence. Non-benevolence asks that near and distant be treated without favoritism; non-action asks rulers to disturb people less with private desire. Neither licenses arbitrary punishment or hidden control.

### Page 12

The difference need not be reduced to which tradition sounds kinder. We can ask three concrete questions: must power give reasons, can the punished appeal, and are rule-makers also bound by rules?

The same ancient word produces different daily lives when placed in different institutions. Recognizing that difference matters more than labeling non-benevolence either compassion or cruelty.
